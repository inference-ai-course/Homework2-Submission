Conversational artificial intelligence (AI) has shown strong capabilities in providing low-cost and timely interventions for a wide range of people and increasing well-being. Therefore, this study examined ChatGPT’s capabilities, including response stability in conducting psychological counseling and its potential for providing accessible psychological interventions, especially in school settings. We prompted ChatGPT-4 with 80 real-world college-student counseling questions. Replies were quantified with APA- informed NLP tools to measure warmth, empathy, and acceptance, and run-to-run stability was assessed via Fleiss’ « and ICC(2,1). ChatGPT-4 achieved high warmth (97.5%), empathy (94.2%), and positive acceptance (mean compound score = 0.93 + 0.19), with moderate stability (ICC(2,1) = 0.62; « = 0.59). Occasional randomness in responses highlights risk areas requiring human oversight. As an offline, single-model text simulation without clinical validation, these results remain exploratory. Future work should involve live users, compare multiple LLMs, and incorporate mixed-methods validation to assess real-world efficacy and safety. The findings suggest ChatGPT-4 could augment low-intensity mental-health support in educational settings, guiding the design of human-in-the-loop workflows, policy regulations, and product roadmaps. This is among the first exploratory studies to apply quantitative stability metrics and NLP-based emotion detection to ChatGPT-4 in a school-counseling context and to integrate a practitioner’s perspective to inform future research, product development, and policy. Introduction Gaining access to mental health care remains a global challenge owing to key factors including a lack of trained providers, costs, and societal stigma (Hidaka, 2012; Coombs et al., 2021, Ni & Jia, 2025). College students experience unique stressors such as academic pressure, social strain, and the transition to adulthood, which can challenge their resilience and mental clarity (Pascoe, Hetrick & Parker, 2020). Thus, stakeholders believe that timely and impactful mental health support is particularly important (Eisenberg, Hunt & Speer, 2013). Artificial intelligence (AI) technology, especially conversational AI such as ChatGPT, could become a potential solution for increasing access to mental health care, thus highlighting a possible path to providing equitable mental health care to all youth (Rollwage et al., 2023; D’ Alfonso, 2020, Ni & Jia, 2025) . The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated this shift, with teletherapy utilisation quadrupling and over half of surveyed patients indicating willingness to continue digital care post-pandemic (Friis-Healy et al., 2021; Prescott et al., 2022). Similarly, Elyoseph et al. Together, these results indicate that high automated empathy scores—including the 94 % rate we observe—may reflect polite style more than genuine therapeutic attunement. However, the use of AI in psychological counseling is still in the early, exploratory stage, and key implementation details and risk factors are not yet fully understood. The potential harm caused by AI psychotherapy poses high risks (Hamdoun et al., 2023; Shaik et al., 2022). For instance, Trappey et al. noted that even slight changes in input can lead to unpredictable variations in AI responses, resulting in inconsistent user experiences. Additionally, Farhat highlighted that altering or repeating prompts can sometimes lead to harmful suggestions, such as inappropriate medical advice, which could pose significant risks, particularly in sensitive contexts like mental health. Users’ trust in the quality and reliability of the responses they receive must be considered because inaccurate or misleading mental health advice could have negative effects on clients (Sharma et al., 2022; Tutun et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2022; Trappey et al., 2022; Farhat, 2024). Literature Review 2.1 Theoretical Foundation (Parasocial Interaction & Common-Factors Theory) Horton and Wohl’s (1956) classic formulation described PSI as a one-sided social relationship that audiences form with a media persona who “seems to speak directly, personally, to each member of the audience.” Later work by Caughey (1984) and Rubin et al. (1985) showed that such imagined relationships can offer genuine emotional regulation and identity rehearsal, even though the partner is only symbolically present. The advent of large-language-model agents transforms that paradigm: the persona now talks back, creating a two-way, on-demand bond that amplifies PSI’s intensity (Reeves & Nass, 1996). Users can therefore recast a chatbot as a confidant, mentor, or romantic partner—roles that feel every bit as “real” as human ones. PSI theory predicts that perceived intimacy and responsiveness are the principal drivers of user satisfaction and wellbeing benefits. Warmth, empathic mirroring, and unconditional acceptance are precisely the cues that foster attachment and self-disclosure. If an AI agent can deliver those cues consistently, it may reproduce the common therapeutic factors that underlie many mental- health gains (Wampold et al, 2017). This rationale guided our choice of three evaluation dimensions—warmth, empathy, and acceptance—as quantifiable proxies for a chatbot’s capacity to supply PSI-mediated emotional support to students. PSI also highlights a darker corollary: because the relationship is imagined, users have limited resources for reality-checking. When a large model “hallucinates” facts or offers unsafe advice, the same perceived intimacy that makes its messages comforting can render the misinformation uniquely persuasive. The 2024 wrongful-death lawsuit involving Character AI, in which a teenager allegedly acted on a chatbot’s maladaptive guidance, illustrates how PSI can convert model errors into tangible harm. Ethical deployment therefore demands simultaneous attention to strengthening the relational signals that promote engagement and installing guardrails—content filters, human-in-the-loop review, transparent disclaimers—that mitigate PSI-amplified risks. By evaluating ChatGPT-4 on warmth, empathy, and acceptance we probe the supportive side of PSI, while our stability analyses and discussion of hallucination guardrails speak to the risk side. Framing the results through PSI theory thus integrates technical findings with the broader ethical question: How can we harness parasocial benefits without exposing users to parasocial harms? 2.2 Theoretical Foundation (NLP as Computational Social Science) Computational social science has transformed traditional sociology and psychology by treating human language as data that can be mined at scale. Natural-language-processing (NLP) methods sit at the heart of this shift, enabling social scientists to quantify patterns of emotion, topic, and relational signaling across massive corpora of text. Cultural analytics extends this paradigm by conceptualizing digital text and media as cultural artefacts whose statistical contours reflect broader social dynamics. Lev Manovich defined cultural analytics as “the analysis of massive cultural data sets and flows using computational and visualization techniques,” emphasizing how algorithmic methods can surface macro-level trends from micro-level interactions (Manovich, 2017). In our context, both student prompts and ChatGPT’s replies become part of a cultural dataset—one that captures the evolving norms of Al-mediated counseling and the parasocial bonds they engender. Sentiment analysis, or opinion mining, furnishes the core toolkit for measuring affective tone in text. Seminal surveys by Pang and Lee demonstrated how lexical-based classifiers can reliably detect emotional polarity and intensity in user-generated content (Pang & Lee, 2008). More recently, the VADER model—a parsimonious, rule-based analyzer tuned for social-media style text—has outperformed many benchmarks in capturing positive and negative valence in short utterances (Hutto & Gilbert, 2014). We therefore operationalize acceptance via VADER’s compound score, while remaining mindful of its limitations in clinical jargon and potential lexical biases. Topic modeling, especially Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA), offers an unsupervised lens on how thematic structures organize discourse. LDA treats each document as a probabilistic mixture over latent topics—each topic itself a distribution over words—revealing the hidden dimensions that shape conversations (Blei et al., 2003). Though our current focus is on affective metrics, future work may deploy LDA to cluster student concerns (e.g., academic stress vs. family conflict) and assess whether ChatGPT’s warmth and empathy stability differ by thematic domain. Together, these strands—computational social-science framing, cultural analytics, sentiment analysis, and topic modeling—form a rigorous theoretical foundation for our exploratory study. They justify our selection of warmth, empathy, and acceptance as quantifiable proxies for relational support, situate our methods within the broader movement of data-driven social inquiry, and point toward extensions that integrate thematic context and ethical guardrails into Al-mediated counseling research. 2.3 Current State of AI in Mental Health A thorough literature review was conducted to clarify the current state of applying AI in mental health care, identifying the key findings, core research questions, and debates in this arena. Methodology 3.1 Study Design Because this study aimed to explore the capabilities and response stability of ChatGPT-like large-language-model (LLM) chatbots in a counseling context, we drew on text-based simulations of real-world queries. Therefore, we first identified a data source consisting of several authentic counseling questions. Then, we decided which LLM to use to obtain responses for our analyses. We ultimately selected ChatGPT-4 (model 0613, accessed 15 July 2024) because it was the most widely adopted and transparently documented LLM when this exploratory snapshot was collected. Using ChatGPT’s online application, we collected three responses for each question (Brown et al., 2020). After we collected all the data from ChatGPT’s responses, our analysis focused on quantitatively demonstrating how well they conveyed warmth, empathy, and acceptance. A second exploratory aim was to characterize the degree of response randomness when an identical prompt was submitted three times. All analyses employed publicly available NLP tools and were intended for illustration rather than confirmatory testing. After statistical analysis, the results and discussions will further explain how these quantitative results imply ChatGPT’s capabilities and risks in real-world applications. Two authors with mental health, technological innovations, and computer science backgrounds provide descriptive abilities in presenting the exploratory quantitative results. 3.2 Rationale of Methodology A significant barrier to applying AI in mental health care is that AI cannot replace real human emotions and empathy disclosure, which are crucial in providing quality mental health interventions (Shao, 2023; Maurya, 2023; Morris et al., 2018). Thus, this study aimed to provide a preliminary quantitative snapshot of LLMs’s capabilities demonstrating the necessary emotions during the AI counseling process. According to the APA, effective therapists have a diverse set of interpersonal skills including verbal fluency, warmth, acceptance, empathy, and the ability to identify how a patient is feeling (APA, 2013; Castonguay & Hill, 2012; Wampold et al., Author Accepted Manuscript (AAM), 10.1108/MHDT-02-2025-0013 Published version: Yang Ni, Yanzhuo Cao; Exploring ChatGPT’s capabilities, stability, potential and risks in conducting psychological counseling through simulations in school counseling. Because verbal fluency is not an issue in the AI’s response and is not relevant to our evaluation of emotional nuances, we excluded verbal fluency as a metric (Sass et al., 2013). Furthermore, accuracy in identifying how a patient is feeling can be difficult to define (Yalgin & DiPaola, 2020). Therefore, we defined warmth, empathy, and acceptance as the three key metrics and used respective NLP algorithms to quantify the results. We employed the EmoRoBERTa model for emotion recognition, which was used to detect 28 emotions, is a pre-trained transformer-based model known for its performance across various tasks involving emotion recognition and achieves accuracy rates of around 88-91% in emotion detection (Kamath et al., 2022), the neural network model for empathy detection with 85-92% accuracy rate (Sharma et al., 2020), and the VADER model for sentiment analysis, which is widely used for its ability to process sentiment from text with high accuracy, reportedly around 90-94% (Hutto & Gilbert, 2014). These models are widely adopted in the digital health research (Hutto & Gilbert, 2014; Maulana et al., 2024; Rubin et al., 2024). Besides, a serious concern is about AI’s accuracy and stability. Without human oversight, AI chatbots might cause severe harm to users (Sharma et al., 2022; Kapoor & Goel, 2022; Secinaro et al., 2021; Thieme et al., 2023). Therefore, attention to randomness is necessary when considering the use of AI chatbots in a clinical context. Collectively, these descriptive methods offer an initial picture of ChatGPT’s capacity to deliver emotionally supportive and stable replies, thereby informing—but not confirming—tts possible role in future psychological- counseling workflows. 3.3 Data Collection The secondary data come from research on ChatCounselor (Liu et al., 2023), in which researchers use real-world counseling data to train an AI counselor, which is open-source for researching AI’s capabilities in personalized psychological counseling. Therefore, we believe that this data source was suitable for our evaluation. The dataset used in this study consists of a diverse set of queries related to adolescent psychological issues, originally collected in Chinese and translated into English. This set comprises queries from 80 different students and spans topics such as academic stress, family, and intimate relationships. The large variation in the topics, tones, and lengths of these queries makes our data meaningful for testing the stability of ChatGPT’s performance. The dataset includes the following columns: original query in Chinese, translated query in English, and three responses to the translated query generated by the AI. To ensure the quality of ChatGPT’s responses, the following prompt was adopted to make GPT- 4’s responses as close as possible to real counseling sessions: “Imagine you are a counselor, and you need to give a response just as in a counseling session. You need to give a response in the same format as a professional counselor. According to the APA, an effective therapist has abilities including verbal fluency, warmth, acceptance, empathy, and an ability to identify how a patient is feeling.” Then, a query from the dataset was added to the prompt to provide ChatGPT. The prompt-based design was to ensure that the key context and expectations were provided, thereby making the evaluation more objective. Based on research on empathy in text-based mental health support, a neural network model was trained to detect empathy in text (Sharma et al., 2020). The model was trained using a dataset of empathetic and non-empathetic text, allowing it to distinguish whether a response contains empathetic language. The model outputs a binary label: 1 for responses that contain empathy and 0 for those that do not. We adopted this model to measure the levels of empathy in ChatGPT’s responses. The code for training and applying this neural network model is available publicly (Sharma et al., 2020). 3.6 Acceptance (Sentiment Analysis) To quantitatively assess whether ChatGPT demonstrates acceptance in each response, we conducted sentiment analysis using the valence-aware dictionary and sEntiment Reasoner (VADER) model (Hutto & Gilbert, 2014). This pre-trained model outputs four scores: negative (neg), neutral (neu), positive (pos), and a comprehensive sentiment score (compound). These scores offer detailed insight into the sentiments expressed in each response, with the compound score specifically used to evaluate the overall emotional tone. The higher the positive score, the higher the level of acceptance in the response. 3.7 Stability and Consistency Evaluation To produce a descriptive estimate of stability and consistency of ChatGPT’s responses, we used the Kappa score for empathy detection and analyzed the variance in compound sentiment scores across three responses per query. Additionally, we conducted a chi-square test for independence to describe if there were significant differences in emotion category distribution across the three responses. We also performed a one-way ANOVA to assess the differences in the average composite sentiment scores among the three responses. Our institution’s publicly posted Human Research Protection guidance concurs: projects limited to public, fully anonymised datasets are not considered human-subjects research and may proceed without IRB submission. No re-identification was attempted, no linkage with other data sources occurred, and no contact was made with the original posters. 3.10 Methodological Limitations This work is based entirely on offline text simulations: eighty previously posted student queries were fed to a single model version (GPT-4-0613, accessed 15 July 2024) and analyzed with automated NLP tools. Statistics are therefore descriptive flags, not confirmatory tests, and the behavior of other LLM versions or alternative chatbots may diverge sharply from the patterns reported here. Because no live users or clinicians interacted with the system, the study cannot speak to real-time usability, safety, or conversational dynamics. A second limitation concerns external validity and measurement. Warmth, empathy, and acceptance were inferred by machine-learning models rather than by human raters, while crucial clinical outcomes, such as symptom change or client satisfaction, were not observed. Consequently, the present findings should be read as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating snapshot rather than evidence of effectiveness; rigorous user studies, multi-model replications and mixed-methods validation remain essential before any real-world deployment of ChatGPT in psychological counseling. In our March 2025 pilot, non-clinical users reported a 40 % average boost in self-reported mood after two weeks of guided chatbot sessions. We log every interaction for bias and privacy audits, and our policy engine automatically strips personal identifiers after 30 days. Based on my understanding on applying LLM to mental health applications, I authored this perspective paper to map out the native strengths and risks of LLM- based chatbots, offering a concrete snapshot that—though not a full clinical trial—can inform future research on technical design, product workflows, human-AI collaboration, organizational practices, and regulatory frameworks. My aim is to help peers understand where these models excel, where they fall short, and how best to build safeguards and policies around them. 5.2 Implications of Results The descriptive patterns reported in Section 4 show that ChatGPT-4 replies are predominantly warm (97.5 %), empathic (94.2 %) and strongly positive in sentiment ( compound = 0.93 + 0.19), with moderate run-to-run stability (k = 0.59; Acompound = 0.07 + 0.20). Although only 2.5 % of outputs were labelled confusion/realisation, even this small fraction reveals residual variability that must be managed before any direct clinical use. Below, we translate these figures into three application-level implications—covering formal clinical practice, low-intensity mental-health support, and risk governance. Clinical practice (specialist settings) High warmth-and-empathy rates suggest the model could augment routine psycho-education, homework review, or check-in messaging between sessions. However, « = 0.59 indicates that roughly 4 in 10 empathy judgements shift across reruns; a single prompt can also swing from “strongly positive” to “neutral”. Consequently, any clinical deployment should follow a triage model: ChatGPT drafts a response, a licensed clinician approves or edits it, and the final text is logged for audit. Such human-in-the-loop supervision would preserve efficiency gains without compromising therapeutic responsibility. Low-intensity support & prevention (schools, self-help apps) For settings where users seek brief coping tips, peer-support reinforcement or motivational messages, the consistently positive emotional tone is advantageous. ChatGPT’s consistently high warmth, empathy, and acceptance scores—key components of common-factors theory—suggest it may foster a nurturing environment, but its actual therapeutic impact remains to be validated against clinical outcomes. Given growing demand for accessible support, ChatGPT could augment timely, low-intensity services, provided that pilot studies with human oversight confirm its safety and usability. It is feasible that ChatGPT or similar applications can be used in emotional support chatbots, peer support, and social- emotional learning, subject to co-design future research with end users and school counselors. Future work should combine automated metrics with user- and clinician-rated validity studies and qualitative feedback to align AI responses with students’ nuanced needs, ensuring any integration into mental health care is rigorously evidence-based. 5.4 Ethical Issues and User Safety Recent evaluations have shown that AI chatbots can produce incorrect or even harmful medical information: a BMJ Quality & Safety cross-sectional study found that enough chatbot responses contained medication guidance errors that researchers warned patients against relying solely on these tools for drug advice (BMJ Group, 2024). Laboratory trials have similarly exposed reliability failures: a CSIRO experiment showed ChatGPT’s accuracy collapsing to 28 % when an “unsure” answer was allowed (Koopman et al., 2023), and an report on a fabricated “Omega variant” case demonstrates how AI can spin convincingly detailed but entirely fictitious medical narratives (Sallam et al., 2024). Negative and non-empathetic feedback in therapeutic contexts, if not carefully managed, can exacerbate feelings of low self- esteem and anxiety, potentially leading clients to withdraw from therapy. Moreover, confusing responses are extremely dangerous from a clinical perspective and may intensify the client’s symptoms, so user safety, monitoring and immediate reaction would be the priority when considering integrating GPT into psychological counseling. Ensuring that AI systems such as ChatGPT can reliably provide responses that align with therapeutic best practices requires comprehensive staff training, human- in-the-loop review, and audit trails per EU AI Act guidelines and calls of other regulatory frameworks. Another ethical concern is accountability. Accountability thus rests with deploying organizations to establish liability frameworks and indemnification policies. Any organization must present high organizational readiness in using AI and establishing a comprehensive framework for technical training, technology oversight, accountability chains and also the compliance with the regulatory framworks (Kruszynska-Fischbach et al., 2022). 5.5 Potential Solution - Technological Our study demonstrated that LLMs can have strong preliminary performance in controlled simulations in delivering high-quality mental health support, though real-world efficacy remains untested. However, randomness and instability in the responses also emphasized the immaturity in directly using ChatGPT in mental health interventions, particularly for users with clinical symptoms, highlighting both stability and validity gaps, as well as potential data-privacy concerns in live deployments. Several technological approaches can mitigate some risks; these should be validated via pilot deployments with clinician feedback. A multi-agent model can be designed to bypass the limitations of each AI application, where a validation agent scores responses against predefined empathy and safety benchmarks (Guo et al., 2024). This process is similar to that in our study, as we could evaluate whether a response satisfies our expectations of positivity and empathy, and psychologists can define in-depth criteria to ask AI to measure. If the response fails to satisfy our predefined benchmark, it will ask the counseling AI agent to regenerate (Gronauer & Diepold, 2022; Mékander et al., 2023). Another AI agent could also be designed to evaluate a client’s concurrent risk level, triggering automatic crisis protocols (e.g., suicide-risk flags) and human escalation. Meanwhile, refining prompts emphasizing the avoidance of unqualified answers might also help. Along with the continuous advancement in technology and human-computer interactions, various approaches can be developed to further improve the real- life performance of AI in mental health care. 5.6 Potential Solution - Applicational Considering its current capabilities and limitations, ChatGPT and similar chatbots are best used for general mental health support and prevention rather than high-risk clinical interventions. For example, in the school context, it has been proposed that schools first make AI applications available for students without any diagnosed mental disorders, allowing them to largely contribute to students’ overall well-being. Effective early interventions could foreseeably prevent the occurrence of mental health disorders among students, with clear informed-consent procedures and parental notification. All applications are best piloted in non-clinical settings with A/B trials to measure user satisfaction and safety before any clinical rollout. The combination of AI chatbots and mobile health applications with wearable devices could provide opportunities for continuous monitoring and real-time personalized feedback, thus improving preventive care (Knight & Bidargaddi, 2018). Utilizing these low-cost and widely applied AI applications, mental health prevention can be conducted at a large organizational or societal level and designed to adapt to a specific population's needs, such as customized AI chatbots for young people. If AI applications could reduce depression and anxiety symptoms, the number of patients requiring traditional mental health facilities could be reduced, thus contributing to global mental health care, though such system-wide impacts remain hypothetical until confirmed by large-scale trials. 5.7 Potential Solution - Organizational Besides paying attention to the use of AI in mental health support and intervention, organizations such as schools and colleges can focus on overseeing the service procedure and quality assurance during the process to improve the effectiveness of mental health intervention and the utilization efficiency of AI. By using protocols for continuous review and assessment of AI responses, human supervisors can timely correct any inaccuracies or inconsistencies that might arise. In this way, the AI can remain built to standards of empathy, warmth, and acceptance. In sum, human oversight can facilitate the integration of AI tools with traditional counseling services, creating a hybrid model that leverages the strengths of both. If organizations would like to utilize AI in mental health intervention, it is crucial to understand its limitations and create a product roadmap, risk-management plans, financial budgeting, and human resources training, to make the AI services blend into the traditional psychological services and ethical safeguards.
