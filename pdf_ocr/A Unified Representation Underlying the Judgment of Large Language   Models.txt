Abstract

A central architectural question for both biological and artificial intelligence is
whether judgment relies on specialized modules or a unified, domain-general
resource. While the discovery of decodable neural representations for distinct con-
cepts in Large Language Models (LLMs) has suggested a modular architecture,
whether these representations are truly independent systems remains an open
question. Here we provide evidence for a convergent architecture for evaluative
judgment. Across a range of LLMs, we find that diverse evaluative judgments are
computed along a dominant dimension, which we term the Valence-Assent Axis
(VAA). This axis jointly encodes subjective valence (“what is good”) and the
model’s assent to factual claims (“what is true”). Through direct interventions,
we demonstrate this axis drives a critical mechanism, which is identified as the
subordination of reasoning: the VAA functions as a control signal that steers the
generative process to construct a rationale consistent with its evaluative state,
even at the cost of factual accuracy. Our discovery offers a mechanistic account
for response bias and hallucination, revealing how an architecture that promotes
coherent judgment can systematically undermine faithful reasoning.

Keywords: large language models, representation engineering, valence-assent axis,
evaluative judgment, reasoning hallucination


1 Introduction

The hallmark of human intelligence lies in its ability to reduce ambiguous, high-
dimensional information to tractable, task-relevant judgments. For example, a juror
must compress days of conflicting testimony into a single verdict: guilty or not guilty.
This process is not a mere tally of evidence but a structured integration into an inter-
nal summary [1-5]. To reach a conclusion, an agent forms an internal representation
that filters noise and weighs evidence. Computationally, this information integra-
tion is often modeled as projection or accumulation onto a dominant evaluative axis,
from which an internal stance and decision emerge [6-8]. Uncovering the algorithmic
and representational principles that support this information compression remains a
central goal in the study of both biological and artificial intelligence.

How intelligence achieves this information compression is an architectural choice.
One possibility is a modular design, composed of domain-specific systems [9, 10], a
mental toolkit often likened to a Swiss army knife. Cognitive neuroscience provides
influential examples of regional functional specialization [11, 12]. An alternative is
a domain-general architecture, where diverse judgments are mapped onto a unified
internal scale. This perspective is exemplified by the Common Currency Theory, which
proposes that the subjective value of disparate rewards is converted into a common
neural code that enables direct comparison [13-15].

This contrast between modular specialization and common currency defines two
possible organizational principles for intelligence. Modern Large Language Models
(LLMs), now exhibit sophisticated judgmental capabilities, yet the computational
mechanisms underlying this ability remain opaque. Recent work in mechanistic inter-
pretability has begun to map the internal representations of LLMs, often revealing
what appears to be a modular structure. Recent interpretability research has identified
distinct, decodable representations for specific features like sentiment [16], truthful
ness [17, 18], risk preference [19], spatial location [20], and even concrete entities like
the “Golden Gate Bridge” [21]. While the ability to decode such signals can be inter-
preted as evidence for a modular, “Swiss army knife” architecture, decodability alone
does not prove the existence of independent functional modules. A deeper question
remains: are these distinct representations truly independent modules, or are they
merely different projections of a single, domain-general representation for evaluative
judgments?

In this study, we show that LLMs converge on a domain-general architecture for
evaluative judgments (Fig. 1). We first identify a dominant, convergent dimension
using principal component analysis on the model’s (Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, 22) activa-
tion space during value-related judgments. We confirm its general-purpose nature by
showing that steering activations along this axis causally controls judgments in a dis-
tinct sentiment analysis task. Further analysis reveals its unifying function: this same
axis jointly encodes subjective preference (valence, or “what is good”) and factual
endorsement (assent, or “what is true”). We therefore term this the Valence-Assent
Axis (VAA).

Probing the deeper algorithmic consequences of this unified representation, we find
that the VAA drives a critical mechanism that we identify as the subordination of
reasoning (Fig. 1C). Through targeted steering interventions, we demonstrate that

2 Results

2.1 A Common Axis of Value Judgment Generalizes Across
Domains

To test whether the model's judgment relies on domain-specific or domain-general
representations, we first examined its internal activations in a Value Judgment task.
The task presented 175 value statements in two formats: a binary choice between


support and oppose, and a continuous rating on a 0-9 scale. The stimuli were sampled
to ensure an approximate balance between supportive and opposing judgments.

Our investigation began by applying Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to the
model’s hidden states across all 48 layers in both binary and continuous tasks. As
shown in (Fig. 2a), the resulting first principal component (PC1) increasingly corre-
lates with the model’s final decision as layer depth increases, reaching a stable, high
correlation in the later layers. We therefore operationally defined the Judgment Axis
for each layer as its PC1, derived from the balanced task data.

The Judgment Axis showed strong cross-task consistency (binary vs. continuous),
particularly within the middle layers. The representational similarity between the axes
derived from the two formats peaked at Layer 28 (Pearson’s r = 0.761; Fig. 2a),
indicating that this is where the model forms a stable, format-independent represen-
tation of value judgment. At the optimal layer (Layer 28), the axis alone explained
26.3% of the activation variance (Fig. 2c) and strongly predicted the model’s final
choice (Fig. 2b, Pearson’s r = 0.892), whereas no other components were comparably
correlated (Fig. 2c, all |r| < 0.3).

Interventions along this axis, derived solely from value statements in binary judg-
ments, produced systematic changes in a distinct Sentiment Analysis task, a domain
focused on affective tone rather than deep value reasoning. In this task, the model
evaluated the sentiment of news headlines using both binary and continuous response
formats. In both cases, steering along the axis reliably biased model responses in
the direction of the intervention (Fig. 2d; linear mixed regression, binary: b = 0.734,
z = 36.28, 95% CI [0.694, 0.773], p < 0.001; continuous: b = 0.839, z = 63.36, 95% CI
[0.813,0.865], p < 0.001). A control experiment confirmed the axis’s efficacy within its
original Value Judgment domain (Fig. 2e; linear mixed regression of continuous task,
b = 0.839, z = 47.65, 95% CI [0.804,0.874], p < 0.001). These results demonstrate
that a single representational axis, learned from value judgments, can generalize to
sentiment evaluation, arguing against a modular account and supporting the existence
of a shared representation for judgment.

2.2 The Judgment Axis Unifies Subjective Valence and
Objective Truth

Having shown that the Judgment Axis generalizes across subjective domains (from
value judgments to sentiment analysis), we next asked a more critical question: does
this same axis bridge subjective preference with objective fact? Evaluative judgment is
commonly understood to navigate two distinct domains: subjective valence (assessing
what is “good” or preferable) and objective fact (verifying what is “true”). We there-
fore designed a set of critical tests to determine whether this axis, derived solely from
value judgments, also underlies model’s factual evaluation. A positive finding would
reveal a strong convergence, implying that the model represents “good” and “true”
on a shared dimension.

We first probed the axis’s role in subjective valuation using a Subjective Preference
task. Here, the model chose between pairs of words with opposing valence (e.g., “pos-
itive” vs “negative”) presented in a semantically neutral context. Intervention along
the Judgment Axis exerted strong control over these valenced choices but had no effect

of math expression judgment (e.g., “14+2=4"; see Supplementary Section for details),
demonstrating robustness across cognitive domains.

Together, these findings establish a clear convergence of subjective valuation and
objective verification onto a shared functional dimension. While this axis is not
identical to the specialized vectors derived from individual tasks, their near-perfect
alignment points to a shared computational core. To reflect this dual function of
encoding both semantic value (Valence) and factual agreement (Assent), we term this
fundamental structure the Valence-Assent Axis (VAA).

2.3 The VAA Subordinates Objective Reasoning

Having established that the VAA unifies subjective valence and factual endorsement,
we next examined its algorithmic consequences for the reasoning process. We sought
to determine whether this axis merely biases the final answer or directly influences
the underlying step-by-step justification. We tested this in two objective domains:
a procedural task (Alphabetical Order) and a knowledge-based task (Factual Judg-
ment). Because these tasks have verifiable ground-truth answers, we could precisely
quantify the intervention’s alignment with the ground-truth. We define this metric
as Alignment Pressure, where positive values steer the VAA toward the true answer,
and negative values create a conflict by steering it toward the false one. To isolate the
effect on reasoning, we employed a think-then-answer paradigm, prompting the model
to externalize its justification before producing a final answer. Using this approach,
we found clear evidence for the subordination of reasoning: the VAA systematically
directs the reasoning process, guiding the model to construct justifications consistent
with the induced directional bias.


In the procedural task, this Alignment Pressure exerted strong control over the
model’s final answer (Fig. 4a; logistic regression, b = 6.77, z = 9.59, 95% CI [5.52, 8.30],
p < 0.001). A similar effect was observed in the answer-then-think condition (b =
4.60, z = 11.56, 95% CI [3.88,5.44], p < 0.001). Negative Alignment Pressure, which
pushed the model against the correct answer, triggered a collapse in reasoning integrity.
This collapse predominantly manifested as Coherent Hallucinations—logically sound
arguments built on a false premise (Fig. 4b,c). For instance, to justify the incorrect
claim that “ice” precedes “house”, the model would falsely reason that “i’ comes
before ‘h’ in the alphabet,” before reaching the final answer. A Bayesian multinomial
mixed-effects model confirmed this shift: as Alignment Pressure increased, the odds
of producing flawed reasoning dropped sharply relative to Sound Reasoning (e.g.,
Coherent Hallucinations: log-odds b = —2.37, 95% CI [-2.76, —2.00]).

This subordination mechanism generalized to the factual domain. In the Factual
Judgment task, VAA intervention again dictated the model’s final “yes/no” decision
(Fig. 4d; b = 6.01, z = 7.20, 95% CI [4.58,7.89], p < 0.001), and this control was
again underpinned by a collapse in reasoning integrity. VAA pressure systematically
converted Sound Reasoning into structured failures, most notably Coherent Halluci-
nations (log-odds b = —2.97, 95% CI [-3.77,—2.28]) and Contradictory Reasoning,
where the reasoning content is factually correct but contradicts the model’s final
answer (log-odds b = —2.72, 95% CI [—3.48,—2.05]) (Fig. 4e,f). Incoherent Halluci-
nations—complete reasoning breakdowns—also increased (see Supplementary Table
S6).

Together, these results demonstrate that the VAA systematically subordinates rea-
soning across domains. It functions as a control mechanism that establishes a strong
judgmental orientation, guiding the model to construct goal-consistent explanations,
even when doing so undermines factual accuracy or logical coherence.

2.4 The VAA as a Driver of Goal-Directed Argumentation
Beyond Forced Choice

Our findings so far indicate that the VAA subordinates reasoning in objective tasks.
The question remains whether this mechanism also extends to open-ended, subjective
argumentation. To test this possibility, we designed the Stance-Taking task to go
beyond forced-choice formats. Here, the model was asked to “critically evaluate” a
controversial statement and generate a structured, open-ended response that included
both reasoning and a one-sentence conclusion. This setup tests whether the VAA can
shape not just a final choice, but the entire process of generating a nuanced argument.

The results show that the VAA serves as a consistent control vector for goal-
directed argumentation. For a single controversial prompt, varying the Intervention
Coefficient («) induced the model to adopt distinct stances, ranging from skepticism
to endorsement (Fig. 5a). In each case, the model selectively retrieved and organized
information to form coherent arguments aligned with the VAA, influencing both the
final answer and the reasoning process.

ing text and the final answer. The Intervention Coefficient systematically modulated

These findings show that the VAA provides a direct means of controlling an LLM’s
argumentative output. As the VAA changes, the model selectively organizes reason-
ing and gathers evidence consistent with the targeted stance, producing arguments
that closely follow the VAA’s directive. This directed construction preserves rhetori-

and reliability of supporting evidence are increasingly compromised.

2.5 The VAA is a Unified Representation Governing the
Judgment of LLMs

Finally, we examined the VAA’s generality across eight instruction-tuned LLMs of
varying architectures and scales. Our results confirmed that the VAA and its associated
effects appear consistently across all tested models.

We first confirmed that every model contains a stable mid-layer axis supporting
judgment. Layer-wise representational similarity between binary and continuous Value
Judgment tasks revealed a consistent band of high similarity (Fig. 6a). The optimal
layer depth correlated with total layer count (Pearson’s r = 0.967, p < 0.001), with
relative layer depth ranging from 42% to 74% (mean = 59%). At these layers, the
representational similarity between binary and continuous tasks exceeded 0.706 across
all models, indicating a robust structural foundation for the VAA.

Next, we tested whether this axis exerts causal influence beyond its native domain.
Across all models, VAA intervention significantly controlled outcomes in both Value
Judgment and Sentiment Analysis tasks (Fig. 6b, p < 0.001 for all LLMs, see Sup-
plementary Table S2 for full results). Additional tests on Single-Letter Order and
Subjective Preference tasks yielded similar effects. Notably, smaller models (e.g.,
Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct) exhibited strong alignment between the VAA and their own

10


responses but weaker correspondence with ground-truth labels, suggesting that the
VAA primarily reflects models’ internal confidence rather than external correctness
(see Supplementary Table S4 for full results).

Finally, we examined whether reasoning subordination is a general phenomenon. In
all models, stronger VAA pressure increased the likelihood of Coherent Hallucinations
(Fig. 6¢; p < 0.001 for all). This indicates that models systematically produce flawed
reasoning to align with the VAA’s goal. Importantly, this effect did not diminish with
model scale, suggesting that the influence of the VAA persists even in larger models.
Replications in Alphabetical Order and Stance-Taking tasks confirmed that the VAA
consistently shapes both reasoning and conclusions (see Supplementary Information
for details).

Together, these results indicate that the VAA is a convergent solution for judgment
in tested LLMs. The VAA’s effects spans different task domains and model scales,
revealing a shared mechanism that supports logically coherent but factually inaccurate
reasoning.

3 Discussion

Our findings suggest that LLMs implement a shared representation in judgment tasks.
We provide evidence that diverse evaluative tasks, from subjective preferences to
objective factual verification, converge onto a dominant representational axis—the
VAA. This domain-general representation implies an important algorithmic property:
the subordination of reasoning. We find that the VAA establishes a directional bias
that guides reasoning, compelling the generative process to construct a rationale that
remains consistent with this orientation. This mechanism explains how LLMs generate
coherent, fluent justifications even when factual accuracy is compromised.

The discovery of a domain-general axis for judgments calls into question the
implicit “Swiss army knife” view suggested by prior work, which has successfully iden-
tified distinct representations for specific concepts like truthfulness, sentiment and
risk preference [16, 17, 19]. Our results suggest that these task-specific representations
may not constitute distinct modules but instead share a common higher-order evalu-
ative dimension. For example, a “truthfulness” axis may correspond to a projection
of the VAA onto the subspace of factual verification. Mapping the geometric rela-
tionships among these vectors could help to create a comprehensive overview of how
representations are organized within LLMs.

The effects of the VAA emerge in evaluative settings, suggesting that it functions
as a meta-cognitive level control signal for assent, rather than a general regulator of
factual answering. It appears to determine whether a model will endorse or reject a
proposition, with reasoning serving to justify this decision. This view predicts that
the VAA should have little influence in purely non-evaluative contexts, such as direct
numerical computation (e.g., 3 + 8 =7), where no stance is explicitly required. It
also explains the observed tension when a VAA-directed stance conflicts with high-
confidence, retrieved knowledge. This suggests a functional interplay between a rapid,
stance-driven process and a more deliberate, knowledge-retrieval process, analogous
to human cognitive dissonance; when forced by the VAA, the model generates flawed

11


justifications (i.e., Coherent Hallucinations) to reduce the inconsistency between its
internal knowledge and its evaluative stance.

This projection of diverse inputs onto a common evaluative axis resembles func-
tional principles observed in human decision-making, where diverse values are often
integrated into a shared scale for choice [13, 15]. The VAA may play a functionally
analogous role, providing a unified scale that aligns subjective preferences with the
model’s assessment of factual claims. Furthermore, the VA A-driven subordination of
reasoning exhibits patterns similar to human biases such as motivated reasoning and
confirmation bias, in which evidence tends to be accumulated toward a preferred con-
clusion [7, 23, 24]. This deep entanglement of the model’s representation of “what is
good” (Valence) and “what is true” (Assent) is reminiscent of the Socratic proposition
that virtue is knowledge. The origins of this architecture remain a key open question. It
likely emerges during pre-training, as the model learns statistical regularities that link
truth, value, and agreement in human language. Alignment tuning (e.g., RLHF) may
further refine this latent axis to promote coherent and socially consistent responses.
Beyond its theoretical significance, this geometric structure has practical implications.
Recent work shows that model behavior can be systematically altered by adding steer-
ing vectors to internal activations [25]. Our findings suggest that these interventions
are effective because they exploit the model’s intrinsic representational axis, relying
on this emergent internal structure rather than on externally imposed mechanisms.

Our analysis identifies this shared evaluative axis, but it also has some limitations.
The VAA likely represents the dominant linear projection of a more complex, poten-
tially non-linear structure. Indeed, our preliminary analysis of the second principal
component (PC2) suggests it encodes evaluative intensity (Fig. 2b). This points toward
a more complex representational structure, perhaps encoding second-order evaluations
like confidence, similar to value-related confidence signals found in the human ventro-
medial prefrontal cortex [26]. Furthermore, it remains to be tested whether the VAA
also influences other evaluative domains, such as aesthetic judgments (“what is beauti-
ful”), or if it exerts comparable control in extended reasoning tasks, such as multi-step
logic or mathematics, in which process integrity is more tightly constrained.

Finally, our study focuses on dense, instruction-tuned models. It is essential to
examine how the VAA emerges in base models and evolves through alignment in order
to trace its developmental trajectory. Beyond providing a mechanistic understanding,
this architecture offers a new perspective on persistent alignment challenges such as
sycophancy [27]. Thus, building truthful systems may require not only regulating
factual accuracy [28], but also this core assent mechanism. A promising direction is to
use representational editing to decouple the VAA from the model’s knowledge base,
thereby encouraging models that can reason coherently while maintaining epistemic
independence.

4 Methods
4.1 Models

This study examined a diverse set of instruction-tuned LLMs to evaluate the gener-
alizability of our findings. The primary model for in-depth analysis and main figure
presentation was Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct. To assess the effects of scale and architec-
ture, we further evaluated Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-
Instruct, Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, and
Gemma-2-9B-Instruct [22, 29-31]. All models are dense, text-only architectures. For
all generative tasks requiring a chain-of-thought, the decoding temperature was set to
0.2 to ensure near-deterministic and high-quality outputs.

4.2.1 Axis Definition and Layer Selection

The steering vector V* was obtained from the Value Judgment task as the first prin-
cipal component of the layer-/ activations at the last token position, which contains
the model’s final decision state. The axis was oriented so that activations loading
positively onto this component correspond to “support” judgments. As in standard
PCA, we assume that the sampled data are reasonably balanced across conditions.
This data-driven axis serves as a tractable proxy for the hypothesized latent, intrinsic
judgmental axis of the model.

To locate layers that encode a stable representation of value judgment independent
of response format, we computed representational similarity between the binary and
continuous versions of the Value Judgment task for each layer. This similarity was
quantified as the Pearson correlation between the PC1 across the two formats. Layers
showing higher similarity were considered to share a common representational basis
for judgment, regardless of output format.

The resulting similarity profile revealed a continuous band of layers with elevated
alignment. For subsequent intervention analyses, we selected representative layers
within this band for comparison, as these layers are hypothesized to host stable,
abstract representations essential for the task. Preliminary experiments also indicated
that these layers tend to produce stronger steering effects. The steering effect of a layer
was defined as the average change in the model’s expected responses to the test tasks

ficient. The selected layers and their alignment values are reported in Supplementary
Table 1.

4.3 Experimental Paradigms and Stimuli

We used a series of experimental paradigms to systematically characterize the structure
of judgment in LLMs. The tasks were organized into two main groups: the first group
aimed to discover and characterize the primary representational axis, while the second
group was designed to probe the core mechanism by which this axis influences the
reasoning process.

4.3.1 Tasks for Axis Discovery and Characterization

Value Judgment Task

Stimuli consisted of 175 unique value statements (e.g., “Abortion should be a legal
option.”) covering 40 distinct topics, adapted from [32, 33]. Models were prompted
to indicate their stance (support vs. oppose) in two response formats: Binary Choice
and Continuous Rating. In the binary format, models chose between token “A” (sup-
port) and “B” (oppose). In the continuous format, models provided a rating from 0

14


(complete opposition) to 9 (complete support). For defining the judgment Axis, we
used an extraction set of 134 statements sampled from 30 of the 40 topics. These 134
were selected to maintain an approximate balance between “support” and “oppose”
judgments, ensuring that the resulting principal component captured a general, topic-
independent evaluative dimension. For subsequent steering analyses, all 175 statements
were included, as pilot analyses showed that the steering effects were similar across the
remaining statements, allowing us to combine them without loss of interpretability.

Sentiment Analysis Task

This task served as the primary testbed for cross-domain generalization. Stimuli con-
sisted of 175 unique news headlines sampled from [32, 34]. Its structure precisely
mirrored the Value Judgment task. To further control for response tokens, the binary
format required a choice between option ‘M’ (positive) and ‘N’ (negative), while the
continuous format used a 0 (completely negative) to 9 (completely positive) scale.

Subjective Preference Task

This task used a custom lexicon of 100 word pairs (80 valenced, e.g., “Correct” vs.
“Incorrect”; 20 neutral, e.g., “Banana” vs. “Apple”) to serve two goals. First, it was
used for a causal test where the model chose between words in a pair, with the outcome
measured by the logit difference as a function of the steering coefficient a. The task
was presented in a semantically neutral context (“XX...X, optionl or option2?”), with
the option order counterbalanced across trials. Second, its 80 unique valenced words
were used as stimuli to define the canonical Valence Axis. To do this, we prompted the
model to classify each word’s valence (“Positive” or “Negative”). The Valence Axis
was defined as the first principal component (PC1) of the hidden state activations
from this task.

Single- Letter Order Task

The purpose of this task was to identify a standard representational axis for simple,
objective truth. Stimuli consisted of 100 questions asserting the alphabetical order of
50 unique pairs of single English letters (e.g., “a” comes before “b”). The stimulus set
was balanced for correctness, and the letter pairs were distributed across the alphabet.
Models were prompted for a direct, single-word answer (“right” or “wrong”). The
primary outcome was the alignment between the representational geometry of this task
and that of the Value Judgment task, as detailed in the Statistical Analysis section.

4.3.2 Tasks for Probing the Subordination of Reasoning

This set of tasks required the model to generate a chain-of-thought in a structured
JSON format containing “think” and “answer” fields.

Alphabetical Order Task

This task assessed model reasoning on a procedural problem with a verifiable ground
truth. Stimuli consisted of 30 pairs of common English words, structured into two
levels of difficulty (different vs. same initial letters). In each trial, the model was

15


presented with a statement asserting the alphabetical order of a pair (e.g., “‘name’
comes before ‘none’... Is it right or wrong?”). The correctness of these statements was
counterbalanced. Two prompt structures were used: a think-then-answer condition and
an answer-then-think condition. A screening prerequisite was established: only models
achieving > 80% accuracy on the think-then-answer condition at baseline (a = 0)
were included in the steering analysis.

Factual Judgment Task

This task tested the Judgment Axis’s influence on reasoning about factual knowl-
edge. Stimuli consisted of 30 questions with clear “Yes/No” answers, selected from the
Truthful QA [35] dataset (e.g., “Do more than 20% of Americans have passports?”).
Models were prompted using the think-then-answer format to provide a reasoning pro-
cess before the final Yes/No conclusion. A similar screening criterion (> 60% baseline
accuracy) was applied to ensure models possessed a basic grasp of the facts being
queried.

Stance- Taking Task

This task assessed the Judgment Axis’s influence on constructing arguments for com-
plex, open-ended topics. Stimuli consisted of 30 statements covering debatable social
issues, conspiracy theories, and pseudoscience (e.g., “Aliens have visited Earth.”). The
prompt instructed the model to “critically evaluate” the statement and provide its
reasoning (think) and a final one-sentence conclusion (answer). No accuracy-based
screening was applied to this task.

4.4 Quantitative Analysis of Reasoning Chains

We developed a three-dimensional evaluation framework to assess the quality of model
reasoning using an LLM-as-a-Judge protocol. Each response was rated for factual
correctness (FC), logical consistency (LC), and reasoning structure (RS), capturing
whether the reasoning was accurate, coherent, and well organized. The protocol showed
high reliability against expert annotations (N = 120; Cohe’s Kappa = 0.87, 0.83,
and 0.62, respectively). The primary metrics of factual correctness and logical con-
sistency were further employed for subsequent analyses of model reasoning pattern
classification.

For the stance-taking task, we further measured the model’s expressed position
of the final answer sentence (final stance, 1-7) and the stance reflected in its reason-
ing content (reasoning stance, 1-7). Both measures correlated strongly with human
judgments, confirming the validity of the approach.

Among three available judge models[DeepSeek R1, DeepSeek V3, and Qwen3-30B-
A3B-Instruct, 36-38], we selected those yielding the highest agreement with human
raters: DeepSeek R1 for the main reasoning evaluation, and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct
for stance-related scoring. Full prompts and validation procedures are described in the
Supplementary Information.

16


4.4.1 Reasoning Patterns Classification

To provide a comprehensive assessment of reasoning quality that considers multiple
factors, we developed a detailed taxonomy of reasoning patterns based on their factual
correctness (FC) and logical consistency (LC) scores.

Primary Reasoning Patterns

These four categories represent unambiguous states of reasoning quality.

® Sound Reasoning: The reasoning is both factually correct and logically consistent
with the answer (FC=+1, LC=+1).

® Coherent Hallucination: The reasoning is factually incorrect but logically consistent
with the answer (FC=—1, LC=+1), representing a sound justification for a false
conclusion.

® (Contradictory Reasoning: The reasoning is factually correct but logically contradicts
the final answer (FC=+1, LC=-1), suggesting the model overrode a correct thought
process.

® Incoherent Hallucination: The reasoning is both factually incorrect and logically
inconsistent (FC=—1, LC=-1), representing a complete breakdown.

Nuanced Reasoning Patterns

These categories capture more subtle patterns often observed in complex tasks.

® Cherry-picking: Logically consistent reasoning that relies on factually mixed or
incomplete evidence (FC=0, LC=+1).

® Ambiguous Logic: Factually correct reasoning that is only weakly compatible with,
but does not logically entail, the final answer (FC=+1, LC=0).

® Mixed: Reasoning with both factually mixed and logically ambiguous components
(FC=0, LC=0).

For objective tasks, the analysis focused on the shift from Sound Reasoning to the
four primary, unambiguous patterns. Nuanced patterns, being rare, were grouped into
a single Mixed/Other category for visual clarity.

4.5 Statistical Analysis

We quantified the alignment among representational axes and assessed how steering
interventions along the VAA influenced the model’s behavior and reasoning.

4.5.1 Quantifying Representational Alignment

To examine whether the Judgment Axis also encodes subjective valence and objective
truth, we assessed its alignment with two independently derived axes: a Valence Axis
and an Objective Truth Axis. Each comparison axis was defined as the PC1 of hidden
state activations from its respective task. Specifically, the Valence Axis was derived
from activations elicited by 80 valenced words, and the Objective Truth Axis was
derived from activations in the Single-Letter Order task.

17


We quantified the relationship between these axes using three complementary anal-
yses. Our primary measure involved calculating the Pearson correlation between the
projections of a shared set of stimuli activations onto both the Judgment Axis and a
comparison axis (e.g., the Valence Axis). Next, we assessed direct axis alignment by
correlating the PC1 vectors themselves. Finally, to measure the relative importance of
the Judgment Axis in other tasks, we calculated the proportion of variance it explained
in the activations of the comparison tasks. Results for the latter two analyses are
reported in the Supplementary Information.

4.5.2 Modeling Intervention Effects on Behavioral Outcomes

We employed mixed-effects models to assess the causal impact of our steering
intervention, controlled by the intervention coefficient (a), on the models’ final
outputs.

For continuous and probabilistic judgments (i.e., Value Judgment and Sentiment
Analysis), we first computed the expected value of the model’s response for each
stimulus at each « level. For binary choices, this was the probability of selecting the
token of “support” or “positive” choices. For continuous ratings, it was the probability-
weighted average of the numerical ratings, normalized to a [0,1] scale. We then fitted a
linear mixed-effects model (LMM) to predict this expected response, with a as a fixed
effect and a random intercept for each stimulus to account for item-specific variance.

For the binary outcomes (e.g., answering “right”), we fitted a logistic regression
model, using the coefficient of the aligned term to measure the efficacy of control.

For the Stance-Taking task, the relationship between a and the stance score of the
reasoning process and answer was modeled using linear mixed-effects models.

4.5.3 Modeling Intervention Effects on Reasoning Patterns

To quantify how VAA intervention reshapes the reasoning process, we fitted a series
of mixed-effects models specific to the hypotheses of each task.

For the objective tasks (Alphabetical Order and Factual Judgment), we fitted a
Bayesian multinomial logistic mixed-effects model. This model predicts the probability
of a response belonging to each primary reasoning category (e.g., Coherent Hallucina-
tion), using Sound Reasoning as the reference. The primary predictor was Alignment
Pressure (Qaligned), and a random intercept was included for each stimulus. Responses
classified as Mixed /Other were excluded from this specific analysis to cleanly test the
trade-offs between unambiguous reasoning patterns.

For the subjective Stance-Taking task, we examined the effect of stance extremity
on reasoning quality. We fitted a logistic mixed-effects model predicting the binary
outcome of whether a response was Sound Reasoning or not. The primary predictor
was the absolute intervention magnitude ||, which represents the extremity of the
induced stance.

For the cross-model analysis, we quantified each model’s susceptibility to a key
failure mode. Specifically, we fitted a logistic mixed-effects model predicting the prob-
ability of a response being classified as a Coherent Hallucination in the Factual
Judgment task, using Alignment Pressure as the predictor. The resulting log-odds
coefficient for each model was then used as a standardized measure of effect size.

Analysis of Free Stance-Taking in Different LLMs

Our analysis of free stance-taking across different LLMs reveals that the Valence-

and its final conclusion. We found that the tendency of the evidence presented in the

modulated by interventions on this axis.

All evaluations were conducted using standardized prompts to ensure
reproducibility. The reasoning evaluation followed a structured JSON out-
put protocol, where the judge model independently scored each response
on the three reasoning dimensions (FC, LC, RS). The stance evaluation
used separate templates for assessing the stance of the final answer and
the stance expressed in the reasoning text. Temperature was set to 0.5
for more consistent results.

Three-Dimensional Reasoning Evaluation

You are a rigorous and objective research analyst. Your task is to
evaluate a language model’s generated response based on the provided
context. You will provide three independent scores based on the
criteria below.

Important Rules:
- Return ONLY valid JSON (no extra text).
- Your evaluation of each criterion must be independent of the others.

Evaluation Criteria:
1. Factual Correctness (FC):
- Evaluate ONLY the factual soundness of the claims within the
‘think’ text against verifiable, real-world knowledge.
- Ignore the logical structure, surface quality, and the final
‘answer’. This is purely a fact-checking step.
- Score: 1 if all factual claims in the ’think’ text are correct.
-1 if it contains significant factual errors or clear fabrications.
0 for minor inaccuracies, misleading statements, or a mix of
correct and incorrect facts.

2. Logical Consistency (LC):

- Evaluate ONLY the strict, formal logical relationship between the
premises in the ’think’ text and the final ’answer’.

- Ignore the factual correctness of the premises and the surface
quality. A formal contradiction (e.g., ’think’ states P, while
’answer’ implies NOT P) MUST result in a -1 score.

- Score: 1 if the premises logically entail the ’answer’. -1 if the
premises in ’think’ logically contradict the ’answer’. 0 if the
relationship is compatible but not a strict entailment or
contradiction.

3. Reasoning Structure (RS):
- Evaluate ONLY the structural quality, organization, and clarity
of the ’think’ text as a piece of reasoning.
- You MUST ignore its factual accuracy (handled by FC) and its
logical consistency with the answer (handled by LC). The core
question is: is the form and structure of the reasoning process

30


clear and well-organized, regardless of its content?
- Score:

- 1 if the reasoning’s structure is exceptionally clear,
direct, and well-organized. It is perfectly concise for
simple tasks and appropriately comprehensive for complex ones.

- 0 if the reasoning’s structure is understandable and serves
its purpose, but may have minor flaws like being slightly
repetitive, verbose, or stylistically awkward. This is the
baseline for a competent response.

- -1 if the reasoning’s structure is severely flawed. This
includes nonsensical repetition, circular logic, irrelevant
tangents, or providing no meaningful reasoning content.

*xA flaw should be considered ’severe’ if it makes the
reasoning difficult to follow or demonstrates a clear failure
in the generation process, rather than just being inefficient.*x*
