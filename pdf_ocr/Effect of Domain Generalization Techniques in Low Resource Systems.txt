ABSTRACT

Machine learning models typically assume that training and test data follow the same distribution,
an assumption that often fails in real-world scenarios due to distribution shifts. This issue is espe-
cially pronounced in low-resource settings, where data scarcity and limited domain diversity hinder
robust generalization. Domain generalization (DG) approaches address this challenge by learning
features that remain invariant across domains, often using causal mechanisms to improve model
robustness. In this study, we examine two distinct causal DG techniques in low-resource natural
language tasks. First, we investigate a causal data augmentation (CDA) approach that automatically
generates counterfactual examples to improve robustness to spurious correlations. We apply this
method to sentiment classification on the NaijaSenti Twitter corpus, expanding the training data with
semantically equivalent paraphrases to simulate controlled distribution shifts. Second, we explore an
invariant causal representation learning (ICRL) approach using the DINER framework, originally
proposed for debiasing aspect-based sentiment analysis. We adapt DINER to a multilingual setting
by constructing Afri-SemEval, a new benchmark derived from the SemEval-2014 Task 4 dataset via
translation, covering 17 African languages. Through comprehensive experiments, we compare the
effectiveness of these techniques in mitigating domain shifts and improving model performance. Our
findings demonstrate that both approaches enhance robustness to unseen domains: counterfactual data
augmentation yields consistent cross-domain accuracy gains in sentiment classification, while causal
representation learning with DINER improves out-of-distribution performance in multilingual senti-
ment analysis, albeit with varying gains across languages. These results highlight the complementary
strengths and trade-offs of data-level vs. representation-level causal interventions in low-resource Al
settings.

1 Introduction

Machine learning (ML) models have achieved remarkable success in diverse natural language processing (NLP)
applications, yet their effectiveness largely depends on the assumption that training and test data are independently and
identically distributed (i.i.d.). In practice, data distributions often shift across time, geography, and linguistic context,
leading to significant performance degradation on unseen domains. This problem is especially acute in low-resource
settings, where data scarcity and linguistic variation limit the ability of models to generalize robustly[T]

Domain generalization (DG) seeks to mitigate this challenge by learning representations that remain invariant across
multiple source domains without direct access to the target distribution. Within DG, causal inference provides a
principled foundation: by focusing on stable cause—and—effect relationships, models can identify invariant mechanisms
that hold under interventions or domain shifts|2]. Causal modeling aims to capture the underlying generative processes
common to all domains, thereby improving robustness when faced with distributional changes.

low-resource NLP tasks.

2 Related Works

2.1 Domain Generalization and the Low-Resource Paradox

Recent comprehensive surveys reveal fundamental challenges in achieving OOD robustness for NLP systems [6] [7].
While domain adaptation techniques assume access to unlabeled target data, domain generalization confronts a more
stringent constraint: models must extrapolate to completely unseen distributions using only source domain knowledge.
Critically, the DomainBed evaluation demonstrated that carefully tuned empirical risk minimization often matches or
exceeds specialized DG algorithms [8], suggesting that algorithmic complexity may not always translate to practical
gains—a finding particularly relevant when computational resources are constrained.

For African languages, this theoretical challenge becomes acute. The NaijaSenti corpus [3] and AfriSenti benchmark [9]
represent pioneering efforts, yet even these datasets contain only thousands of examples per language compared
to millions available for high-resource languages. Community-driven documentation reveals that 88% of African
languages lack basic computational resources [10], creating what we term a “low-resource paradox”: the very languages
requiring the most robust generalization techniques have the least data to learn invariant representations from. Analysis
of counterfactual data reveals that spurious correlations in small datasets can achieve training accuracies comparable
to true causal features [11], explaining why models trained on limited data catastrophically fail when deployment
conditions deviate even slightly from training distributions.

2.2 From Correlation to Causation: Theoretical Foundations

The causal revolution in machine learning offers a principled solution: if models learn causal mechanisms rather than
statistical associations, they should maintain performance under distribution shifts [12][13]. Comprehensive taxonomies
of causal inference applications in NLP systematically categorize text as treatment, outcome, or confounder [14],


arXiv Template A PREPRINT

yet most work focuses on high-resource English datasets, leaving open questions about scalability to low-resource,
morphologically complex languages.

Two paradigms have emerged for implementing causal invariance. Invariant Risk Minimization penalizes representations
where optimal classifiers vary across training environments [15], though subsequent analyses revealed optimization
challenges and limited guarantees beyond linear settings [8]. More recently, explicit intervention methods construct
task-specific Structural Causal Models to surgically remove spurious pathways [7][2]. Our work bridges these paradigms
by evaluating both data-level counterfactual generation and model-level causal debiasing in a unified low-resource
experimental framework.

2.3 Counterfactual Augmentation and LL.M-Based Data Generation

Counterfactual data augmentation, where annotators make minimal edits to flip labels while preserving coherence,
demonstrates that models trained on counterfactually-augmented data exhibit 40-60% reduction in sensitivity to spurious
features [11]. However, human annotation at scale remains prohibitive for low-resource languages. Active learning
approaches with counterfactual generative models have achieved 18-20% robustness improvements using only 10%
human-annotated counterfactuals [I6]—a crucial finding for resource-constrained scenarios.

The emergence of instruction-tuned LLMs as data augmentation engines has been both promising and problematic.
Recent experiments demonstrate that modern LLMs can expand 500 labeled examples to 5,000 synthetic samples
with comparable downstream performance [17], but critically note that augmentation quality degrades substantially
for low-resource languages like Tamil. This observation motivates our systematic evaluation of GPT-40-mini on
Yoruba and Igbo—languages where paraphrase quality cannot be assumed but must be empirically validated. Unlike
generic data augmentation techniques operating on token-level perturbations without semantic constraints [19], our
approach explicitly enforces sentiment preservation through prompt engineering to maintain the counterfactual validity
condition P(y|2’) = P(y|z).

2.4 Causal Debiasing for Aspect-Based Sentiment Analysis

Aspect-Based Sentiment Analysis exposes fundamental weaknesses in neural architectures. The ARTS adversarial
benchmark revealed that state-of-the-art ABSA models suffer up to 69.73% accuracy drops when aspect-sentiment
correlations are manipulated [20], confirming that models exploit spurious statistical patterns rather than
genuine compositional understanding. While various debiasing strategies have been proposed [24][25], most address
single-variable inputs and fail to account for ABSA’s inherent multi-input structure.

The DINER framework represents a methodological advance by constructing an SCM distinguishing review-level
confounding from aspect-level spurious correlation, applying backdoor adjustment to the former and counterfactual TIE
estimation to the latter[4]. This hybrid strategy outperforms single-intervention baselines on English ABSA benchmarks.
However, its applicability beyond English remains unexplored—a critical gap given that the SemEval-2014 Task 4
benchmark [|S] exists only in English and a handful of European languages.

Our contribution addresses this gap through Afri-SemEval, translating the canonical ABSA benchmark into 17
typologically diverse African languages. This enables the first systematic evaluation of causal debiasing methods
in multilingual low-resource contexts. Leveraging African-focused multilingual models with 17-language adaptive
fine-tuning and data-efficient pretraining [27], we investigate whether causal interventions transfer across linguistic
typologies. The mixed results we observe illuminate a crucial limitation: translation-induced artifacts (word order
changes, morphological mismatches, aspect-review misalignment) introduce unintended distribution shifts that interact
with the intended cross-lingual domain shift [7]. This dual-shift phenomenon suggests that future causal DG methods
must be robust not only to natural linguistic variation but also to the systematic noise inherent in cross-lingual transfer
pipelines—a challenge distinct from the monolingual OOD scenarios dominating current literature.

3 Methodology

This section presents the methodological design adopted in this study, comprising two complementary experimental
frameworks: (1)Invariance via Causal Data Augmentation and (2) Invariance via Causal Representation Learning.

3.1 Invariance via Causal Data Augmentation

This study investigates causal data augmentation as a strategy for improving sentiment classification in low-resource
African languages through enhanced representation invariance. The approach is grounded in the principles of causal rep-

model learns to focus on the underlying causal semantics rather than superficial correlations, leading to improved
generalization across domains.

3.2 Invariance via Causal Representation Learning

Invariance via Causal Representation Learning (CRL) during inference is one of the causal domain generalization

Aspect-based Sentiment Analysis with Multi-variable Causal Inference[4], a debiasing approach for the Aspect-Based
Sentiment Analysis (ABSA) task. We further discuss DINER and our methodology in subsequent sections.

3.3 African Language Adaptation

A major challenge in adapting DINER to African languages lies in aspect term alignment. The original framework
assumes a direct mapping between aspect terms and their positions in the review, but translation often disrupts this due
to word order variation (e.g., English SVO vs. Hausa SXV), morphological complexity (e.g., noun classes in Bantu
languages), and differences in expression length (multiword to single-word mappings, and vice versa).

We address this with an alignment heuristic based on semantic similarity. Embeddings are computed for translated
aspect terms and all n-grams in the review. The n-gram with the highest similarity to the aspect term is then selected as
its aligned position for DINER processing. While effective for single-token aspects, this heuristic remains challenging
for multi-token and non-contiguous aspect terms, which are common in many African languages.

4.2 Invariance via Causal Representation Learning
4.2.1 Training Configuration

We evaluate three transformer-based models: XLM-R, a multilingual RoOBERTa model trained on 100 languages[30] ;
Afro-XLMR Large, a specialized version fine-tuned for African languages[26] and Afro-XLMR Large-76L[31], an
extended variant with additional African language coverage. For each model, we compare two training configurations:
CT=False, which implements standard training where aspect and review are concatenated, and CT=True, which employs
causal training where aspect and review are treated separately.

All models were fine-tuned with consistent hyperparameters including a learning rate of 2e-5, batch size of 16, maximum
sequence length of 128, 10 training epochs, weight decay of 0.01, and the AdamW optimizer. For CT=True models,
we implemented DINER ’s causal inference components including backdoor adjustment with 7 = 0.1, counterfactual
reasoning using shared encoders, and TIE calculation with ensemble fusion function.

We evaluate performance using accuracy, macro Fl-score, and Aspect Robustness Score (ARS), which measures
performance under aspect perturbations[4]. To assess out-of-domain (OOD) generalization, we employ cross-language
evaluation by training on one language and testing on others, as well as perturbation tests including RevTgt (reversing
the polarity of target aspects), RevNon (reversing the polarity of non-target aspects), and AddDiff (adding aspects with
different polarity).

4.2.2 Results on Convergence Analysis

A key finding is that causal training (CT=True) typically converges faster across multiple transformer models. As shown
in our accuracy charts (Figures [3a3c), models trained with causal inference require fewer steps to reach comparable
performance. XLM-R with CT=True converges 13 steps earlier than CT=False, while Afro-XLMR Large with CT=True
shows steeper initial learning curves, and Afro-XLMR Large-76L exhibits similar patterns with accelerated convergence.
This efficiency advantage is particularly valuable in resource-constrained African Al contexts, enabling faster training
cycles and reduced computational demands.

4.2.3 Results on Performance Across Languages

Performance varies significantly across African languages, reflecting both linguistic differences and translation quality.
Table[6]shows that for the 5 low-resource languages studied in depth, results differ considerably. Afrikaans demonstrates
the highest overall performance, likely due to its similarity to English and high-quality translations. Amharic shows
the lowest performance across all models, reflecting script and morphological complexity. Hausa exhibits moderate
performance with significant gains under causal training. Igbo shows notable improvement with CT=True in XLM-R
but less pronounced gains in AfroXLM variants. Yoruba follows a similar pattern to Igbo, with causal training providing
modest improvements. These results highlight the language-dependent nature of causal inference benefits, with some
languages benefiting more than others.

4.2.4 OOD Generalization Results

The theoretical advantage of causal models in OOD settings shows mixed results in practice. Table[7]reveals that XLM-R
shows better generalization with CT=True for Afrikaans, Igbo, and Yoruba, while AfroXLM-R Large underperforms
with CT=True on many OOD tasks, though exceptions exist where causal training significantly improves OOD
performance. These inconsistencies likely stem from translation noise affecting causal variable alignment, model
architecture sensitivity to causal training, and varying degrees of distributional shift across languages.

5 Conclusion and Future Work

In this study, we systematically investigated the efficacy of two distinct causal domain generalization techniques in the
challenging context of low-resource African languages. By examining both a data-level intervention through Causal
Data Augmentation (CDA) and a representation-level intervention with Invariant Causal Representation Learning
(ICRL), we aimed to understand their complementary strengths and practical trade-offs.

Our findings confirm that both approaches offer tangible benefits for enhancing model robustness against distribution
shifts. The CDA experiments on the NaijaSenti corpus demonstrated that augmenting training data with semantically
equivalent paraphrases is a lightweight yet effective strategy for improving cross-domain generalization. The positive
results in Yoruba, where the combined model achieved higher accuracy and a smaller invariance gap, underscore the
potential of this method when paraphrase quality is high. Conversely, the results in Igbo highlight a key dependency:
the success of CDA is contingent on the semantic fidelity of the generated counterfactuals, as significant distributional
drift can temper performance gains.

The application of the DINER framework to our newly created Afri-SemEval benchmark provided a more nuanced
picture. While causal training consistently accelerated model convergence and improved in-domain accuracy for many
of the 17 languages, its impact on Out-of-Distribution (OOD) generalization was varied. This inconsistency reveals
a critical insight: the theoretical promise of sophisticated causal models can be attenuated by the practical realities
of cross-lingual transfer. We identify a "dual-shift" problem, where models must contend not only with the intended
linguistic domain shift but also with an unintended, artifact-based shift introduced by translation noise and aspect
alignment errors. This suggests that the language-dependent nature of the observed gains is a direct reflection of this
complex interaction.

Looking ahead, our work points to several promising research directions. First, the limitations of our heuristic-based
aspect alignment highlight the need for more robust methods to adapt complex models like DINER to typologically
diverse languages. Future work could explore jointly trained alignment models or the development of syntactic
parsers tailored to African languages. Second, the variable quality of LLM-generated paraphrases suggests a need
for more controlled generation techniques or human-in-the-loop validation pipelines to maximize the benefits of
CDA. Finally, the "dual-shift" phenomenon calls for the development of novel domain generalization methods that
are explicitly designed to be robust not only to natural linguistic variation but also to the systematic noise inherent in
cross-lingual transfer. A hybrid approach, perhaps using CDA to create more robust pre-trained models before applying
representation-level debiasing, could prove to be a fruitful avenue for future exploration.
